Section 4: Example Exam Description

This is a fine-grained, massive rock that contains several extensional fractures filled with a white mineral, likely calcite, which is not present in the surrounding host rock. These structures are classified as veins.

The dominant vein set forms clear en-echelon arrays, and many of these veins show a pronounced sigmoidal shape. This geometry indicates that, after the initial formation of the extensional fractures, the rock continued to deform under progressive shear. During this deformation, the central parts of the veins were rotated in the shear direction, developing curved, S-shaped outlines, while the vein tips continued to grow in the original fracture orientation.

These structures are therefore interpreted as sigmoidal tension gashes, which are typical features of brittle–ductile shear zones. They record an overall sinistral shear sense.

Because the vein tips preserve the original opening direction, they provide a reliable indication of the stress field at the time of fracture formation:
• σ₁ (maximum compressive stress) is parallel to the vein tips.
• σ₃ (minimum principal stress) is perpendicular to the extensional opening direction.
This combination of extensional fracturing followed by continued shearing is characteristic of rocks that undergo deformation under mixed brittle–ductile conditions.
